## 🇨🇳 China Moves India Hasn't Matched Yet

### 1. One campus with more power than India's entire data centre fleet

**What China did.** Envision's Galaxy Campus in Ulanqab, Inner Mongolia, is a single 120,000 sq m site designed for up to one million AI accelerators and **over 2GW** of power, sited there specifically for cheap wind and a cool climate.

**Where India stands.** India's *total national* installed data centre capacity is tracking to roughly **1.7–2.0GW by end-2026** across every operator and city combined. The pipeline is real — about 1.3GW under construction, Google's Vizag build approaching 1GW across three campuses, Reliance's Jamnagar site with ~120MW live in H2 2026 and a stated path to gigawatt scale — but one Chinese campus is currently specced above India's entire installed base.

**Why it matters.** The interesting part isn't the raw number, it's the **siting logic**. Ulanqab works because compute was moved to where stranded renewable power already is, instead of dragging power to where the developers are. India has the same asset — Gujarat and Rajasthan renewable corridors with curtailment problems — and Jamnagar shows Reliance gets it. What India lacks is a *national* policy that treats curtailed renewable power as a compute-siting input rather than a grid problem. That's a policy lesson available for free.

### 2. Telcos actually monetising inference, not just announcing it

**What China did.** China Telecom's Xingchen TokenHub carries 142 LLMs, 420+ industry applications and supports 20+ chip architectures; intelligent computing revenue rose 95 per cent in H1 2026. China Unicom's UniAI links 200+ models against 41.9bn yuan of compute revenue. Both now sell **token bundles to ordinary mobile subscribers**, the way they sell data packs.

**Where India stands.** Jio has said the right things — targeting the lowest cost per token per watt globally, Jamnagar capacity, an edge compute layer — and Airtel bundled Perplexity Pro to its subscriber base. But these are announcements and bundles, not a live multi-model token marketplace booking billions in revenue. India has the *distribution* (Jio's subscriber base dwarfs any Chinese carrier's addressable AI market) and not yet the *product*.

**Why it matters.** This is the gap most likely to close fast, and the one with the biggest prize. Whoever ships a credible token marketplace on Indian telco rails gets AI distribution to 400m+ users on existing billing relationships. If you're building AI products in India, assume this arrives within 18 months and design for it rather than against it.

### 3. Memory fabs — a structural, not incremental, gap

**What China did.** YMTC has broken into the global top three NAND suppliers by bit shipments with 14 per cent share, and its parent CCSH is going for what may be China's largest chip IPO, with nearly US$5bn earmarked for capacity and R&D.

**Where India stands.** Micron's Sanand plant is genuinely operational and shipping DRAM and NAND modules at 14 million units a week — but it's **ATMP: assembly, test, packaging**. The wafers come from elsewhere. Tata-PSMC's Dholera fab targets first silicon around December 2026 at 28nm–110nm logic nodes, which is not memory.

**Why it matters.** Be honest with yourself about this one: it isn't a gap India closes by being clever. Memory fabs are $10bn+, brutally cyclical, and the incumbents have thirty-year learning curves. The actionable read for a software founder is the inverse — India's comparative advantage is above the silicon layer, and memory cost inflation (see also Nvidia's 15 per cent price rise) is an **input cost you should hedge in your architecture**, not a business you should enter.

### 4. Open weights as export: models nobody uses aren't leverage

**What China did.** Harvey — backed by OpenAI, Sequoia and a16z — abandoned closed Western frontier models and post-trained its flagship legal system on Moonshot's open-weight **Kimi K3**. Separately, African firms like Nigeria's Curacel are routing production workloads across GLM-5.3, DeepSeek, Qwen and Kimi on cost grounds.

**Where India stands.** India does have open-weight sovereign models: Sarvam open-sourced Sarvam-30B and Sarvam-105B in February 2026, trained end-to-end on IndiaAI Mission compute, and BharatGen shipped Param2-17B. So "India has no open models" is **false** and I won't claim it. What's absent is any evidence of a foreign firm — American, African or otherwise — choosing an Indian base model for a commercial product.

**Why it matters.** The gap is **distribution and credibility, not capability**. Chinese labs turned open weights into a soft-power channel: publish, get adopted abroad, become the default substrate. India's models are aimed inward at Indic-language sovereignty, which is a legitimate goal but doesn't generate ecosystem lock-in. If Indian labs want geopolitical leverage from open weights, they need a benchmark story a foreign CTO can defend to their board — and right now Kimi and Qwen have one.

## 🤖 AI Monetization Opportunities for India

### 1. A model-routing and inference-cost layer for Indian AI companies

**The signal.** Three of today's stories point at the same thing from different angles: Harvey switching its whole stack to a cheaper open-weight Chinese base; Curacel in Nigeria explicitly running multi-vendor routing "based on quality and cost without... locking ourselves into one vendor"; and Nvidia pushing hardware prices up 15 per cent, which flows straight into inference pricing.

**The concept.** A drop-in inference gateway for Indian SaaS and B2C companies that classifies each request by difficulty and routes it to the cheapest model that clears a quality bar — open-weight Qwen/Kimi/Sarvam on rented Indian GPUs for extraction, classification, summarisation and support; frontier Claude/GPT/Gemini only for the genuinely hard reasoning. Ship it with shadow-evaluation (replay real traffic against candidate models before switching), per-route quality regression alerts, and a spend dashboard the CFO can read.

**Indian market & monetization.** Target the ~2,000 Indian SaaS companies and D2C/fintech platforms now running material LLM spend — anyone above roughly ₹5 lakh/month on API bills feels this. Price as a percentage of demonstrated savings (15–20 per cent) rather than a flat SaaS fee: it's an easier sale because it's self-funding, and it aligns you with the customer. Land with a free audit that replays a week of their logs and shows the number.

**Feasibility.** Strong. Pure software, small team, no regulatory exposure, no capex. The moat is the evaluation harness and accumulated routing data, not the proxy itself. Main risk: the model providers or an incumbent gateway (LiteLLM, Portkey — the latter already Indian) commoditise this. Move fast on the *evaluation* piece, which is the hard, defensible half.

### 2. An Indian-law domain model on open weights — the Harvey Tenet playbook

**The signal.** Harvey, the best-funded legal AI company in the world, concluded that a post-trained open-weight base beat frontier closed models on specialised legal work — on both cost *and* accuracy. That's a template, and it's explicitly reproducible.

**The concept.** Post-train an open-weight base (Kimi K3, Qwen, or Sarvam-105B if the Indic-language handling holds up) on Indian judgments, statutes, GST and income-tax circulars, and standard contract forms. Not a chatbot — a workflow product: contract review against Indian Contract Act norms, GST notice triage with citation, due-diligence document review, and precedent search that actually cites the SCC/High Court reporter correctly.

**Indian market & monetization.** Per-seat subscription to in-house legal and compliance teams at mid-to-large Indian corporates and to Tier-2 law firms — the segment that can't afford a large associate bench and currently under-serves compliance. ₹8,000–20,000 per seat per month is defensible against the cost of a junior associate. Chartered accountancy firms are a parallel and possibly easier beachhead, since GST work is higher-volume and more formulaic than litigation.

**Feasibility.** Moderate. The engineering is tractable for a small team. The two real risks are **data rights** — judgment text is public but the good citation apparatus is proprietary to legal publishers, so budget for licensing or build the citation layer yourself — and **liability positioning**: sell it as drafting and review assistance with mandatory human sign-off, never as advice. Hallucinated citations have already ended careers in other jurisdictions; the product must make verification structurally unavoidable, not optional.

### 3. Field-failure early warning for Indian auto OEMs and component suppliers

**The signal.** Tesla was forced into a record 3-million-car recall in China over door handles, with Xiaomi pulling 400,000 — and Beijing is moving to ban the design outright. Separately, Chinese carmakers are hitting a PCB and MLCC shortage because AI demand is eating the supply, squeezing margins on exactly the "intelligent" features they're competing on.

**The concept.** An AI system that ingests dealer service-ticket free text, warranty claim records, parts-replacement rates and public complaint channels (social, consumer forums, the government's own complaint portals), and flags an emerging defect cluster weeks before it becomes a recall. The hard technical problem is real and interesting: service tickets in India are written in mixed Hindi/English/regional transliteration, which is precisely where an Indic-capable model earns its keep and a generic English model fails.

**Indian market & monetization.** Sell to Indian OEMs and Tier-1 suppliers — Tata Motors, Mahindra, Bajaj, TVS, Ola Electric, and the EV two-wheeler segment where thermal and battery field failures are an active reputational problem. B2B enterprise contracts, ₹50 lakh–2 crore annually, justified against a single avoided recall or a shortened detection window. The EV two-wheeler makers are the better entry point: newer, less entrenched IT, and an urgent, well-publicised failure problem.

**Feasibility.** Moderate. Software-only, but this is a long enterprise sale into conservative manufacturers, and you need a design partner willing to hand over warranty data before you have a product. Expect 9–12 months to first real contract. Do not attempt this without one committed OEM design partner from day one — the data is the product, and you can't synthesise it.

---

*Two things I deliberately did not claim: that India lacks open-weight foundation models (it has them — Sarvam-30B/105B, Param2-17B), and that India has no AI data centre buildout (there is roughly 1.3GW under construction). The gaps above are about scale, productisation and external adoption, not absence.*
