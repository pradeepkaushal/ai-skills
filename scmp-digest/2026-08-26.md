## 🇨🇳 China Moves India Hasn't Matched Yet

### 1. Legal liability assigned for fully autonomous driving

**What China did:** The NPC Standing Committee began reviewing an amended Road Traffic Safety Law that makes the carmaker legally responsible for violations and accidents while an autonomous system is engaged — and, in exchange, permits companies to build and sell such vehicles. It draws a statutory line between full autonomy and driver assistance.

**Where India stands:** This is a clean gap. The Motor Vehicles Act 1988 assumes a human driver is at fault; there is no statutory concept of manufacturer liability for an engaged autonomous system. MoRTH's ADAS guidelines are still in draft and BIS's IS 16833 is in development. No Level 4 vehicle is approved for commercial deployment in India. Tata and Mahindra ship Level 2 ADAS at best.

**Why it matters:** The liability rule is the actual unlock, not the technology. Until India answers "who pays when the software crashes the car", no OEM will ship autonomy here and no insurer will price it — which means India's autonomous-driving software talent will keep building for foreign markets. This is a low-cost policy fix India could copy almost verbatim; it costs nothing in capital and unblocks an entire industry.

### 2. Writing the rulebook, not just the product — IEC solid-state battery standard

**What China did:** SAMR's proposal for the world's first international standard on EV solid-state batteries was approved as an IEC work item on 21 August, with China leading and France, Japan and South Korea contributing. The scope extends to humanoid robots, drones and electric aircraft. A Chinese standards researcher put the point bluntly: Chinese products going overseas "will more easily meet the requirements".

**Where India stands:** India is a standards *adopter* here, not a setter — BIS's lithium-ion certification (IS 16046) mirrors IEC 62133. On the underlying manufacturing, the ACC battery PLI has delivered roughly 1.4 GWh against a 50 GWh target, essentially all of it Ola Electric, and no incentives had been disbursed as of early 2026. India is not positioned to lead a solid-state standard because it has barely built conventional cell capacity.

**Why it matters:** Standards leadership is a durable, low-capital form of industrial power — whoever writes the test conditions decides whose products pass cheaply. India's absence from the room is more consequential than the cell-capacity shortfall, because standards get locked in once and last decades. Worth watching whether BIS pushes for convenor roles on IEC/ISO working groups in adjacent areas (EV charging, battery swapping) where India actually has domestic scale to justify a claim.

### 3. BeiDou as measured industrial infrastructure, not just a chipset mandate

**What China did:** MIIT reports BeiDou now covers ~90% of "key industry" applications, with 39 pilot cities adding 2.96m terminals in H1 2026 (+30% YoY) to pass 12m total — 4.6m of them in industrial manufacturing. Several cities report 100% fitment across shared bikes, taxis, government fleets and agricultural machinery.

**Where India stands:** Partly matched, and I'd be careful with this one. India *does* have NavIC — BIS rules require NavIC support in devices sold in India from 2026, and NavIC-capable chipsets are already mandated in parts of the commercial vehicle segment. What India does not have is China's second half: a funded pilot programme that instruments specific industrial sectors and publishes deployment numbers. India mandates the receiver; China deploys and measures the application.

**Why it matters:** The gap is programme design, not satellites. A positioning constellation only pays off when someone builds the industrial application layer on top — precision agriculture, fleet compliance, construction machinery telemetry. India has the constellation and now the chipset mandate, so the missing piece is the commercial layer. That is a software problem, which makes it addressable by exactly the kind of team the reader could assemble (see opportunity #2 below).

### 4. Advanced packaging for AI accelerators — India has OSAT, but not this kind

**What China did:** JCET, Tongfu and Huatian committed over 15bn yuan (US$2.2bn) to new capacity aimed specifically at advanced packaging for AI accelerators — JCET's 7.8bn yuan Lingang plant, Tongfu's memory capacity expansion off a 4.2bn yuan placement. All three are funding this from record profits, not just subsidy.

**Where India stands:** This is *not* the clean gap it looks like at first glance, and the honest version matters. India brought three assembly/packaging plants online in 2026 alone: Micron's ATMP at Sanand (Feb), Kaynes Semicon's OSAT (Mar), and the CG Power–Renesas–STARS OSAT (Jul), with a second CG facility due by year-end. India has real, operating packaging capacity. The distinction is what kind: India's plants are doing memory ATMP and conventional/legacy OSAT, while China's new money is going into 2.5D/3D advanced packaging for AI accelerators — the harder, higher-margin end that sits next to HBM integration.

**Why it matters:** India is roughly one technology generation behind in packaging, not absent from it — a meaningfully better position than the usual telling. The risk is that India's ecosystem consolidates around commodity packaging exactly as demand concentrates in AI-accelerator packaging. The lever is whether the next round of ISM approvals targets advanced packaging specifically rather than more of the same. Worth tracking as a signal, not panicking over.

### 5. Brain-computer interfaces: a standards roadmap before a market exists

**What China did:** Named BCI one of six "industries of the future" in the 15th five-year plan and published a concrete roadmap — 40+ domestic standards by 2028 with 100+ adopting companies, 80+ by 2030, and leadership or participation in 10+ international standards. Neuracle already has approval for an implanted system restoring hand movement in paralysed patients.

**Where India stands:** Implantable BCI research in India is limited, with no domestic implant programme at a comparable stage. India has the regulatory institutions — CDSCO, ICMR, DHR, BIS — but the recognised obstacle is the path from lab research to first-in-human trials to commercial manufacturing, which nobody has walked here.

**Why it matters:** Lower urgency than the other four — this is a decade-out market and the capital and clinical-trial requirements put it outside what a software founder can attack. Flagging it because the *pattern* is the lesson: China is writing standards for a market that doesn't exist yet, which is how you end up owning it. Cheap to copy, and India's institutions already exist.

## 🤖 AI Monetization Opportunities for India

### 1. A cost-routing gateway for Indian software teams' LLM spend

**The signal:** Open-weight Chinese models went from 28% to 62% of traffic on Vercel's AI Gateway in two months, with DeepSeek-V4-Flash in the top two slots and GLM-5.2 and Step 3.7 Flash close behind. The stated driver is pure cost on token-hungry agent workloads — SCMP's related reporting frames tokens as an emerging corporate currency. Meanwhile Beijing's own 2030 plan leans on embodied AI and manufacturing AI, so cheap inference is about to get cheaper still.

**The concept:** A model-routing and cost-governance layer aimed at Indian SaaS companies, GCCs and IT services firms. For each request it picks the cheapest model that clears a quality bar the customer defines, falling back to a frontier model only when needed. The defensible parts are India-specific: evaluation sets covering Indic-language quality (where a cheap open model may fail in ways an English benchmark never reveals), DPDP Act data-residency routing so regulated customers can force traffic to India-hosted weights, and per-team INR budget enforcement with chargeback. Self-hosted open weights are a routing target, not just an API.

**Indian market & monetization:** Sell to engineering and platform teams at Indian SaaS firms (Zoho-scale down to Series A), and separately to IT-services and GCC delivery orgs where AI spend is now a line item on client P&Ls. Price on value share — a percentage of measured savings against a baseline — for the first year, converting to a per-seat or per-million-token platform fee. The IT-services channel is the bigger prize: those firms need to *prove* margin on fixed-price AI contracts, which is exactly what this instrumentation gives them.

**Feasibility:** Strongest of the three. Pure software, small team, no hardware, no regulated licence. Two real risks: the frontier labs and cloud providers ship routing themselves (differentiate on the India-specific eval and residency layer, which they won't build), and customers self-build a thin version in a week (which is why the evals, not the proxy, are the product).

### 2. A NavIC-native fleet and farm-machinery telematics layer

**The signal:** BeiDou's 12m+ pilot terminals — 4.6m industrial — with entire city fleets of taxis, government vehicles and agricultural machinery at 100% fitment. Paired with India's own position: NavIC support is now mandated in devices sold in India from 2026, so the receiver base is arriving whether or not anyone builds on it.

**The concept:** Software on top of India's incoming NavIC receiver base for two segments where positioning data is currently wasted. For agri-machinery: tractor and harvester rental operators (a large, fragmented, cash-based market) get utilisation tracking, geofenced billing per acre worked rather than per hour hired, and theft recovery. For commercial fleets: driver-behaviour scoring and route adherence, with the AI layer doing anomaly detection on movement patterns rather than dashboard-building. The wedge is that NavIC gives regional accuracy at low cost and the mandate removes the "will there be receivers" question.

**Indian market & monetization:** Per-vehicle-per-month SaaS to tractor rental aggregators and mid-size logistics fleets — the segment too small for enterprise TMS vendors and too big for a WhatsApp group. A second revenue line sells anonymised utilisation data to equipment OEMs and to insurers pricing commercial vehicle risk, though the insurance channel takes longer than founders expect.

**Feasibility:** Medium, and the honest caveat is hardware. The software is buildable by a small team, but you will need an OEM or telematics-device partner for anything not already NavIC-fitted, which means a hardware partnership and inventory risk you can't code your way out of. Agri-rental customers are also famously slow-paying. Start with fleets that already have devices, use agri as expansion once the software is proven.

### 3. AI document adjudication for Indian state e-governance

**The signal:** Hong Kong's "Easy Licence" system will auto-approve over 90% of online vehicle licence renewals, cutting processing from ten days to one. Alongside it, China's Shenzhen smog plan uses AI for source attribution in a compliance context — the same underlying pattern of AI replacing manual review inside a government workflow.

**The concept:** Middleware that ingests a citizen application plus its supporting documents, verifies them against authoritative sources, and auto-approves the clean majority while routing genuine exceptions to a human with the discrepancy already highlighted. India has unusually good rails for this — DigiLocker, Aadhaar-based eKYC, and Account Aggregator all provide machine-readable source-of-truth that most countries lack, which means the hard part (verification) is partly solved and the unsolved part (adjudication logic and exception handling) is where an AI layer earns its keep. Vehicle licence renewals, trade licences, building-plan approvals and scholarship disbursements are all the same shape.

**Indian market & monetization:** Sell per-transaction to state IT departments and municipal corporations, or through the existing GovTech system integrators who already hold those contracts. Per-approval pricing is the right model because it aligns with the department's actual pain (backlog) and survives budget scrutiny better than a licence fee.

**Feasibility:** The technology is the easy half. Flagging clearly: government sales cycles run 12–24 months, procurement favours incumbents with past performance credentials a new company won't have, and an auto-approval system that gets an approval wrong is a political liability, not a bug ticket — expect to be held to a much higher error standard than a private customer would impose. Realistically this is a partner-led play (build the layer, sell through an SI) rather than a direct-to-government startup, unless the founder already has that channel.
