## 🇨🇳 China Moves India Hasn't Matched Yet

### 1. Compute treated as a national utility, not a procurement line item

**What China did.** The "six networks" programme puts a national *computing network* on the same footing as the water grid, the power grid and the logistics network — six coordinated build-outs with roughly 7 trillion yuan (US$1tn) budgeted for 2026 alone, and water/grid/pipelines projected past 16tn yuan through 2030. The NDRC this week convened ministries to accelerate 109 flagship projects and pull in private capital.

**Where India stands.** India plans infrastructure well — Gati Shakti and the National Infrastructure Pipeline are real coordination machinery — so this isn't "India has no infrastructure programme". The specific gap is compute. IndiaAI's total mission outlay is ₹10,372 crore (~US$1.2bn), and its common compute pool has ~38,000 GPUs onboarded with a target of 100,000 by end-2026. That's a subsidised access scheme, not a utility network with a grid interconnect plan behind it.

**Why it matters.** China is building compute the way it built rail: as a shared substrate other industries are expected to plug into. India is buying GPU-hours. The former creates downstream cost advantages for every AI company in the country for a decade; the latter creates a queue. If you're building an AI product in India, assume your inference costs stay structurally above your Chinese counterparts' and design for efficiency rather than scale.

### 2. Single-site AI data centres larger than India's entire installed base

**What China did.** ByteDance is expanding one cluster in Ulanqab, Inner Mongolia, to 5–6 GW — an estimated US$24bn build targeted for early 2028, in a region already hosting Huawei, Alibaba, Kuaishou and Apple.

**Where India stands.** India's total operational data centre IT load was roughly 1.3–1.5 GW in early 2026, forecast to reach 1.7–2.0 GW by year-end. Reliance's Jamnagar project is the flagship — Meta's first build-to-suit block there is 168 MW, with GW-scale ambitions and a reported 3 GW aspiration. So one Chinese company's *incremental* expansion at a single site is roughly three to four times India's entire current base.

**Why it matters.** India's binding constraint here is power, land and transmission, not capital — Reliance's numbers show the money exists. The practical read for a founder: don't build a business whose economics depend on cheap domestic Indian compute in the next three years. The genuinely open opportunity is the layer *above* the metal — routing, caching, efficiency — which is exactly where a small team can compete.

### 3. Solid-state batteries at industrial scale while India is still failing to build conventional cells

**What China did.** Chery is putting 10bn yuan (US$1.49bn) and 1,200 engineers into solid-state batteries at 400 Wh/kg — double current EV cell density — with testing from 2027. No one has mass-produced a solid-state pack yet, so this is a race for the next platform, not a catch-up move.

**Where India stands.** This is the widest gap on the list, and it isn't at the frontier. Under the ACC PLI scheme, 40 GWh of capacity has been *awarded* to four firms, but only 1.4 GWh had actually been established as of 31 May 2026 — about 3.5% — and no beneficiary has yet claimed incentives. Solid-state work in India remains at lab and pilot scale, with fewer than five facilities producing kilogram-scale solid electrolyte. The government issued fresh bids for another 10 GWh (grid-scale storage) in August, closing 13 October 2026.

**Why it matters.** India is not one generation behind on batteries; it is behind on *execution* of the previous generation while China moves to the next. For a software founder this is mostly a signal about which "India will win at X" narratives to discount — but it also means the battery-adjacent software layer (cell diagnostics, second-life valuation, fleet degradation modelling) sits in a market that will be import-dependent for years, which changes who your customer is.

### 4. Domestic semiconductor *equipment*, not just fabs

**What China did.** AMEC unveiled six chip-making machines in a single day; Hua Hong is putting US$2bn into new capacity via a joint venture with the national IC fund. Morgan Stanley's analysis of 229 Star Market IPOs found 20% of 2026 listings target "chokepoint" technologies, up from 8.1% in 2022 — and 19 of the 21 chokepoint firms are in semiconductor supply chains, concentrated in raw materials, complex machinery and core manufacturing tools.

**Where India stands.** India is genuinely moving here, and it would be wrong to call it absent: 12 projects approved under the India Semiconductor Mission across six states, ~₹1.64 lakh crore committed, and Tata–PSMC's Dholera fab past halfway with first silicon targeted for December 2026 at 28–110nm. But the entire programme is fabs and packaging. There is essentially no domestic semiconductor *equipment* industry — the layer AMEC represents.

**Why it matters.** Fabs are the visible prize; tools are where the leverage sits, because a fab without domestic tooling is still export-control-exposed. China concluded this after four years of US controls and redirected its startup and IPO pipeline accordingly. India's chokepoint map hasn't been drawn yet, let alone funded.

### 5. A decade-old state venture machine with exits, versus a one-year-old one with a governance problem

**What China did.** The national SME fund, launched September 2015, now exceeds 120bn yuan, has spawned 46 sub-funds, backed over 2,000 companies, produced 92 IPOs, exited 200+ positions and returned roughly 15%. Phase two is now being launched specifically for IPO-ready early-stage hard tech.

**Where India stands.** India does have state venture capital — SIDBI's Fund of Funds has been deploying since 2016 — so the honest framing isn't absence. The gap is hard-tech focus and track record. The ₹1 lakh crore RDI Fund was approved in July 2025, opened its first call in February 2026, and made its first disbursement in May 2026: ₹2,192 crore in soft loans to 22 firms in space, energy storage, semiconductors and robotics. It also drew immediate criticism — reportedly 15 of the 22 recipients, holding ~62% of the money, have investment ties to members of the selection panel (the government says those members recused themselves).

**Why it matters.** China's fund has ten years of exit data and a sub-fund architecture that puts allocation decisions at arm's length from the state. India is starting the same journey with more money and less insulation. For a deep-tech founder in India the practical implication is that the capital is now genuinely available at 3–4% over 12–15 years — worth applying for — but expect the process to be relationship-mediated for at least a few cycles.

## 🤖 AI Monetization Opportunities for India

### 1. An LLM spend control plane for Indian enterprises and GCCs

**The signal.** Two stories converge. DeepSeek cut V4-Pro pricing 75% in May 2026, and Chinese open-weight models are surging on Western platforms — inference is commoditising fast, with real quality differences between tiers. Meanwhile ByteDance employees are burning ~1bn tokens a month and *budgeting* them, treating token spend as a managed corporate resource.

**The concept.** A routing and governance layer that sits between an enterprise's applications and every model it uses. For each request it picks the cheapest model that clears a task-specific quality bar, enforces per-team token budgets, caches aggressively, and — the part that actually sells it — runs an eval harness so a team can swap a US frontier model for a cheaper open-weight one and *see* whether output quality moved. Include Indic-language evals, because that's where the naive "just use the cheap model" assumption breaks and where nobody has good benchmarks.

**Indian market & monetization.** Target Indian IT services firms and the ~1,800 global capability centres in Bengaluru, Hyderabad and Pune — organisations running millions of low-complexity requests where an 80% routing saving is a board-visible number. Price as a per-seat platform fee plus a share of verified savings; the savings share gets you in the door, the platform fee is the durable revenue.

**Feasibility.** High. Pure software, small team, no regulated data required if you deploy in the customer's VPC. Be clear-eyed about the risk: AWS, Azure and the model vendors will all ship basic routing natively. Your defensibility is the eval corpus — particularly Indic — not the router itself, so build the benchmark first and the routing second.

### 2. Real-time voice-scam interception for Indian telcos and banks

**The signal.** Hong Kong phone scam losses rose 56% year-on-year in H1 2026 to HK$900 million, with impersonating customer service the single most common tactic — and total scam *counts* fell, meaning the attacks got more effective, not more numerous. Separately, China's cyberspace watchdog is scrubbing AI deepfakes across 2,400 platforms. Both point the same way: synthetic-voice social engineering is scaling faster than defences.

**The concept.** A low-latency model that scores a live call for known social-engineering script patterns in Indian languages — bank customer service impersonation, KYC-update pretexts, "your parcel is held by customs", digital-arrest scams — and surfaces a warning during the call rather than after. Two deployment shapes: carrier-side for telcos, or an SDK inside a bank's own app for calls it originates and can verify.

**Indian market & monetization.** Per-subscriber-per-month licensing to telcos, or per-seat to banks and NBFCs for outbound-call verification. India's digital-arrest and UPI fraud losses are large and politically salient enough that RBI and DoT pressure creates a buying trigger, which matters more than product quality for a first contract.

**Feasibility.** The modelling is tractable — compact Indic ASR plus intent classification, not frontier-scale work. But be honest about the two hard parts: telco procurement is a 12–18 month sales cycle, and live-call analysis sits squarely inside DoT/TRAI interception and privacy rules, so this needs regulatory counsel from day one, not after the pilot. A consumer-only app is the weak version — Android call screening is gatekept and you'd have no distribution.

### 3. A production stack for Indian-language vertical micro-dramas

**The signal.** Chinese "vertical drama" platforms — bite-size, phone-native soap operas — are pulling large audiences in the US and disrupting streaming economics. The format is proven and it travels.

**The concept.** Not another streaming app. Sell the pipeline: script generation against the proven retention beat structures the format depends on, AI dubbing and lip-sync across 8–10 Indian languages from a single shoot, and automated hook/thumbnail A-B testing scored against actual retention curves. One production, ten language markets, with the language versioning costing near zero.

**Indian market & monetization.** SaaS plus per-minute processing fees to regional production houses and the OTT platforms already commissioning short-form (Pocket FM's video push, Kuku TV, Amazon MX Player). India's dubbing market is already large and mostly manual; you're attacking a cost line that exists today rather than inventing demand.

**Feasibility.** The tooling is buildable by a small technical team. The temptation is to run your own consumer app instead, because that's where the coin-purchase revenue ceiling sits — resist it unless you want to be a content company. That version is a marketing and content-slate business with brutal CAC, and it is not the thing the reader of this digest is set up to win at. Sell picks and shovels.
