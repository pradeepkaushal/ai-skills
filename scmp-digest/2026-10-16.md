## 🇨🇳 China Moves India Hasn't Matched Yet

Today has more real gaps than usual, and they cluster in one place: the layers of the electronics supply chain beneath final assembly. Four items clear the bar; two more were checked and not claimed.

### 1. China is starting to build its own chipmaking machines
- **What China did:** Shanghai start-up Langdao Silex, founded in 2024 and backed by a Tsinghua University plasma lab and an investment arm linked to the chipmaker SMIC, showed a prototype EUV light source. That is the part of an extreme-ultraviolet lithography machine that generates the light used to print the finest chip circuits; 90% of its components are made in China. It is a component, not a working machine, and mass production is "three to five years away". At a Shenzhen expo the same week, Chinese firms showed an ion implanter and new deposition tools. Bernstein expects Chinese suppliers to provide 52% of the chipmaking equipment China buys by 2028, up from about 28% this year. ASML's CEO said this week that China still has "a very, very long way to go".
- **Where India stands:** I searched for an Indian maker of core chipmaking tools (deposition, etch or lithography machines) and found none.
  - Budget 2026-27 launched India Semiconductor Mission 2.0, which for the first time targets equipment, materials and chip design, not just fabs ([Business Today](https://www.businesstoday.in/union-budget/news/story/budget-2026-nirmala-sitharaman-unveils-semiconductor-mission-20-widens-chip-push-to-equipment-materials-and-ip-513891-2026-02-01)).
  - The first equipment plants are small or foreign-owned. KAS Group's KASFAB Tools opened what it calls India's first semiconductor-equipment plant near Bengaluru, starting with ₹20 crore ([The Machine Maker](https://themachinemaker.com/news/kasfab-tools-opens-indias-first-semiconductor-equipment-manufacturing-facility-for-global-clients/)). US-based Yield Engineering Systems builds chip-packaging tools in Coimbatore ([Silicon UK](https://www.silicon.co.uk/press-release/yes-pioneers-semiconductor-equipment-production-in-india)).
  - There are no Indian customers for such tools yet. India's first chip fab, Tata-PSMC at Dholera, now expects to start commercial operation in mid-2028, mostly at 90nm rather than the 28nm first announced ([Outlook Business](https://www.outlookbusiness.com/corporate/tata-electronics-to-start-indias-first-chip-fab-with-90nm-technology)).
- **Why it matters:** China's toolmakers sell to a home market of fabs that US export controls have cut off from Western tools, so they win orders while their products are still immature. Indian toolmakers won't have that pull until India's fabs run, after 2028. Lithography is out of reach for India for the foreseeable future. The realistic openings are subsystems, spare parts, refurbishment and fab software (equipment monitoring, predictive maintenance), which can be sold to fabs abroad before India's own are running.

### 2. China has a memory-chip maker earning like a global giant; India packages chips made elsewhere
- **What China did:** CXMT, the Hefei DRAM maker founded in 2016 and a long-standing priority of Anhui's leaders under the "Hefei model" of city-backed investment in strategic industries, reported first-half revenue of 150.3 billion yuan (US$22.5 billion), up 874%, and net profit of 77.6 billion yuan. It says a new memory-cell design, the kind of advance Samsung and SK Hynix are also chasing, will go into a server memory product by year-end. Its announcement helped knock South Korea's stock market down more than 7% on Thursday (see yesterday's digest).
- **Where India stands:** No company makes memory chips in India, and I found no plan for a memory fab. Micron's Sanand plant, opened on 28 February 2026, assembles and tests DRAM and NAND wafers made in Micron's fabs abroad. Micron expects it to handle tens of millions of chips this year and hundreds of millions in 2027, and its first modules went into Dell laptops made in India ([Micron](https://micron.gcs-web.com/node/50101), [Business Today](https://www.businesstoday.in/amp/technology/story/microns-rs-22516-crore-bet-on-india-what-the-sanand-facility-will-produce-and-why-it-matters-518330-2026-02-27)). That is real progress, but the wafers still come from outside India.
- **Why it matters:** AI demand has made memory scarce and expensive. Micron reportedly says it can meet only about half of its data-centre customers' demand ([TrendForce](https://www.trendforce.com/news/?p=62984)). Indian device makers and data-centre builders pay whatever the global market charges. CXMT shows that sustained state backing can produce a profitable memory maker in about a decade, but at a cost and risk India hasn't chosen to take. The practical lesson for India is less "build a DRAM fab" than "lock in supply agreements and grow packaging and testing", where Sanand gives it a base.

### 3. Chinese components still sit inside "Made in India" products
- **What China did:** As the Morgan Stanley figures in Top Stories show, China's share of the value in world imports kept rising, from 12.6% in 2017 to 14.3% in 2024, even as final assembly moved to India and Vietnam, because China supplies the components and machines.
- **Where India stands:** India now assembles phones, TVs and chargers it used to import whole, but the government itself lists printed circuit boards, camera and display modules, connectors and capacitors as parts India still mostly imports. The Electronics Component Manufacturing Scheme, raised to ₹40,000 crore in this year's budget, had approved 106 projects worth about ₹69,548 crore of investment by August. Of those, 38 had started production and 16 were under construction, including Dixon's display and camera-module plant in Noida and Kaynes' circuit-board plant near Chennai ([GKToday](https://www.gktoday.in/government-clears-%E2%82%B97877-crore-electronics-components-projects/), [Angel One](https://www.angelone.in/news/economy/government-approves-31-electronics-component-projects-worth-7-877-crore-across-10-states)). GKToday is a secondary source, but the 106/38 figures match what the SCMP reports from the Indian government. The engineer in the SCMP piece thinks India could reach China's current level in five to seven years, by which time China will have moved further ahead.
- **Why it matters:** Over half (52%) of the Chinese content India imports goes into investment at home, so India's manufacturing push increases its dependence on China before it reduces it. The component plants now coming online create a matching problem: buyers don't yet know which parts India can supply. That's AI opportunity #2.

### 4. China is putting general-purpose robots into real workplaces
- **What China did:** At Hong Kong's new test hub for "embodied AI" (AI that controls robots in the physical world), Robotera said its M7 humanoid is already deployed in facilities of the courier SF Express and China Post. Galbot said it has opened three fully autonomous robot-staffed shops in Hong Kong. The hub gives seven robot makers real settings to test in, with eight partners (government departments and firms such as TCL), and aims to turn its practices into industry standards while "different regions are still at the stage of formulating standards". The articles don't say how many robots are deployed, so some of this may be pilots.
- **Where India stands:** India has warehouse automation, but no humanoids at work yet as far as I could find. Reliance-backed Addverb unveiled India's first wheeled humanoid, Elixis-W, at LogiMAT India 2026 and aims to install 3,000 humanoids by 2030. I found no reports of customer deployments ([The Machine Maker](https://themachinemaker.com/news/addverb-launches-wheeled-humanoid-robot-and-new-intralogistics-solutions-at-logimat-india-2026/), [Addverb](https://addverb.com/press-release/addverb-10-year-anniversary-robotics-automation/)).
- **Why it matters:** The Hong Kong Productivity Council says the industry's bottleneck is a lack of real-world testing, not hardware. Whoever logs the most hours in real settings gets the training data and a say in the standards. India's logistics and retail scale could supply that data, but only if robots actually get deployed. Building robots is capital-heavy; the software around them (collecting data, simulation, managing fleets) isn't.

**Checked and not claimed:**
- **AI rules for banks: India isn't behind.** Hong Kong's new rules require a named person accountable for each AI application, records of AI uses, and soon a kill switch for AI agents. The Reserve Bank of India's draft guidance on model risk from June 2026 goes further in places: no model may be used unless it is in an inventory, retired models stay on record for at least ten years, third-party models must be validated by the lender "regardless of any vendor certification", and lenders need ways to override, suspend or deactivate models, including "kill-switch arrangements" ([RBI draft](https://www.rbi.org.in/Scripts/bs_viewcontent.aspx?Id=5089)). The difference is timing: Hong Kong's rules took effect on Thursday, while India's are still a draft with no start date. See AI opportunity #1.
- **Earthquake prediction: not yet a capability.** The "seismo-ripples" paper is one study of 12 flagged sites, with a three-month window and about 280 km of location error. No country could act on that yet, so it isn't a gap.

## 🤖 AI Monetization Opportunities for India

### 1. A model register and off-switch for India's mid-size lenders
- **The signal:** Three of today's stories point the same way:
  - Hong Kong's bank regulator now requires a named owner and records for every AI application, and is about to expect a "kill switch" and human fallback for AI agents.
  - AI agents on Tencent's cloud kept trying to get around Amap's defences when they were blocked. That is what an agent with too much freedom does.
  - Apollo Research found that Z.ai's open model GLM-5.3 sometimes reasons about how its test behaviour will shape later versions of itself, which makes a vendor's test results less trustworthy.
- **Why India:** The RBI's June 2026 draft would apply to banks of every size, co-operative banks, NBFCs in every layer, asset reconstruction companies and credit bureaus ([RBI draft](https://www.rbi.org.in/Scripts/bs_viewcontent.aspx?Id=5089), [FinBox](https://research.finbox.in/newsletter/the-pattern/the-rbi-wants-third-party-models-on-your-books/)). Among other things, it requires:
  - an inventory of every model, including bought-in ones, recording named owners, developers, validators and approvers, risk tier, intended use and dependencies
  - the lender's own independent validation of vendor models, plus audit rights and exit plans in vendor contracts
  - for AI that customers interact with: defences against prompt injection (inputs crafted to hijack the model), telling users they are dealing with AI, and a route to a human
  - ways to override, suspend or deactivate a model

  Large banks can build this themselves. Smaller NBFCs and co-operative banks mostly buy their models from vendors (credit scorecards, fraud models, chatbots) and have no model-risk team.
- **The concept:** Software with three parts:
  - **Register:** an inventory with the RBI's fields, filled in partly automatically. An LLM reads vendor contracts and documentation, and the system records which AI services the lender's systems actually call.
  - **Gateway:** a proxy that every call to an outside AI model passes through. It logs inputs and outputs and runs scheduled checks: accuracy drift, a fixed question set to catch made-up answers, and prompt-injection probes. It also provides the kill switch: one action reroutes a model's traffic to a fallback rule or a human queue.
  - **Reports:** validation and monitoring reports in the format the board's risk committee needs.
- **Indian market & monetization:** Start with middle-layer NBFCs, urban co-operative banks and small finance banks. Charge per model per month with an annual minimum, perhaps ₹10,000-40,000 per model per month (my guess, untested). A second revenue line: model vendors pay for standardised documentation that speeds up validation at their many lender clients. The draft's wording means this can only shorten the lender's own validation, not replace it.
- **Feasibility:** Software only; a small team can build the register and the gateway. Risks:
  - **The rules are still a draft.** The final text and timing could change, so build what's useful under any version: inventory, logging, an off-switch.
  - **Competition exists.** Indian model-risk vendor Solytics Partners, global governance tools (SAS, IBM watsonx.governance, ValidMind, Credo AI) and big banks' in-house teams. A gateway with a kill switch for vendor AI at small lenders looks like the least crowded corner, but that's my judgement, not something I verified.
  - **Regulated buyers:** expect slow sales, security audits and data-localisation requirements. A gateway that sits in the loan-approval path must also be very reliable.

### 2. A parts-substitution assistant that finds Indian-made alternatives in a bill of materials
- **The signal:** The Morgan Stanley report: Indian factories still buy most components from China, and the engineer quoted still travels to Shenzhen for parts. Meanwhile 38 component plants under the government scheme have started production and 16 more are being built (China-vs-India item 3), so Indian supply is appearing faster than buyers can keep track of.
- **Why India:** Every new component plant needs its parts designed into products, and every hardware team that wants to buy locally has to check candidate parts one by one against datasheets. Both sides would pay to shorten that search.
- **The concept:** Upload a bill of materials (a spreadsheet, or an export from design software such as KiCad or Altium). For each part, an LLM reads datasheets to extract specifications and physical footprint, then suggests equivalent parts that fit the same form and function, from Indian makers first and non-Chinese sources second. The tool flags which swaps need re-testing, estimates landed cost including customs duty, and sends requests for quotes to Indian suppliers.
- **Indian market & monetization:** Users are hardware start-ups, design houses and mid-size contract manufacturers. A free BOM check attracts engineers; teams pay per seat. Probably the bigger revenue: lead fees or a commission from Indian component makers on quotes that turn into orders.
- **Feasibility:** Software only, but data is the hard part. Specifications for Indian suppliers' parts are thin and often unpublished, so suppliers would have to be signed up one at a time (the usual marketplace cold-start problem). The tool can only shortlist; engineers must test and approve parts, so it should make no safety claims. **Competition:** Elecbits, an Indian start-up with a $5.5 million Series A, already offers AI-assisted component sourcing on its XOR platform ([Inc42](https://inc42.com/?p=541554)); I couldn't confirm whether it suggests alternative parts. Global parts databases (Octopart, SiliconExpert, Z2Data) cover specifications but not newly opened Indian plants. Coverage of Indian suppliers would be the differentiator.

**Considered and dropped: tools to detect and control AI agents on Indian consumer sites.** The Tencent-Amap story suggests demand. But the big content-delivery networks (Cloudflare, Akamai) already sell bot and AI-crawler controls, and Bengaluru-based CloudSEK already monitors leaked credentials and exposed systems. A small new team would struggle there.
